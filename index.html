        Making a good print

        1. Frame the shot

        Search for a place, then drag the plate to move it and shift-scroll to resize. Match view snaps the plate to whatever is on screen. Somewhere between 800 m and 3 km across gives you recognisable streets at a sensible print size.

        2. Watch the thinnest street

        Streets are widened to a printable minimum so they do not vanish when the map is shrunk to 150 mm. Set Thinnest street to your nozzle diameter — 0.4 mm nozzles want about 0.85 mm so the slicer can lay down two walls.

        3. Colours are parts, not paint

        Every colour is a separate solid. The plate is carved so no two parts overlap, which means a five-filament print and a single-colour print come from the same geometry. Export 3MF for multi-material, or the per-layer STL bundle to assign filaments manually.

        4. When the map data is thin, bring your own

        OpenStreetMap coverage outside city centres is often machine-traced: every building an identical rectangle, none of them with a height. That is a data problem, not a print problem, and it is usually fixable — most councils and counties publish building footprints on an open-data portal, frequently LiDAR-derived and carrying real heights.

        Open Your own data and drop the file in. GeoJSON, KML, KMZ and zipped Shapefiles all work. Pick which column holds the height and whether it is metres, feet or storeys — the tool guesses, and reports the range it read so you can see at once whether it picked the right one. Set Replace here and your footprints take over from OSM's wherever your data covers, leaving the rest of the plate alone.

        Everything is parsed in your browser and kept on your device. If the data is in a State Plane or UTM grid — most government data is — include the .prj in the ZIP and it will be reprojected; without one the upload is refused rather than dropped in the wrong hemisphere.

        5. Buildings are estimates

        OpenStreetMap knows exact heights for some buildings and level counts for many more. Where it knows nothing, height is inferred from the building type. Use Height scale to push the skyline up if it reads flat.

        6. Printing notes

        • No supports needed — everything sits on a flat base.
        • 0.12–0.16 mm layers keep the lettering and kerb lines readable.
        • Print the base a couple of layers thick before the streets start.
        • Skip the brim; the plate has plenty of bed contact.

        “My slicer says thousands of non-manifold edges”

        That is expected for the colour 3MF, and it is not a leak. Each coloured layer is its own closed solid, and where two of them meet they each carry the wall between them — so that shared face has four triangles around it instead of two. Mesh checkers count every one of those and report a large number.

        What matters is open edges, and there are none: each part is watertight, which is what a multi-material slicer needs in order to assign a filament per part. Let it “repair” if it offers to; the result is the same.

        If you would rather not see the warning at all, export the single-file STL and print in one colour, or load the per-layer STL bundle, where each file is separately closed.

        Where the data comes from

        Buildings, roads, water and parks come from OpenStreetMap via Overpass. Elevation is Copernicus DEM through Open-Meteo. Routing is Valhalla. Nothing is uploaded anywhere — the model is built in your browser.

        Known limits

        • Coverage is only as good as OSM in that neighbourhood.
        • Bridges print as solid roadbed, not as spans.
        • Terrain is 90 m resolution, so it captures hills rather than kerbs.
        • Very large areas can hit the public Overpass rate limits — wait a minute and retry.
